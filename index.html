1.3Systems science from the book

Where to intervene in a system

Twelve leverage points, ranked from weakest to strongest. The deeper you go, the more a change moves the whole system, and the harder that change is to make.

Leverage point

12

Numbers

Easier to changeGreater leverage
  1. 12

    Numbers

    Constants and parameters such as subsidies, taxes and standards. The most visible and the least effective.

    Takes more than 75% of our attention
  2. 11

    Buffers

    The size of stabilising stocks relative to their flows: reserves, inventory, savings.

  3. 10

    Stocks and flows

    The physical structure and its intersections. Powerful at design time, slow and costly to change once built.

  4. 09

    Delays

    The lag before effects appear. Long delays cause oscillation, overshoot and collapse.

  5. 08

    Balancing feedback

    Self-correcting loops that keep important stocks within safe bounds, like a thermostat.

  6. 07

    Reinforcing feedback

    Self-amplifying loops, the source of growth, explosion, erosion and collapse.

  7. 06

    Information flows

    Who has access to information. Delivering feedback where it was missing is cheap and powerful.

  8. 05

    Rules

    Incentives, punishments and constraints. The deepest malfunctions sit in the rules and in who holds power over them.

  9. 04

    Self-organisation

    The power to add, change and evolve system structure. In social systems we call it innovation.

  10. 03

    Goals

    The purpose the system is driving toward. Change the goal and every action follows.

  11. 02

    Paradigms

    The mindset from which the system arises. For one person it can shift in a split second.

  12. 01

    Transcending paradigms

    Staying unattached to any one worldview, and reflexive toward all knowledge.

1.4Complexity science from the book

The patterns that pull a system

Phase space is the space of the possible, every state a system can occupy. Attractors are the shapes its behaviour keeps returning to.

After Cohen and Stewart, 1995, and Gharajedaghi, 2006

Point attractor

Drawn toward one thing

The system is drawn to, or repelled from, a single activity. Unidirectional, like the pull of fear, desire or self-interest.

Hover the field to release new trajectories.

Cyclic attractor

Oscillating between states

The system swings between two or more activities, such as stability and change, or security and freedom. Self-maintaining.

Start a trajectory anywhere. It settles onto the same loop.

Torus attractor

Organised complexity that repeats

Equifinal and goal-seeking, reaching the same end by many paths. Typical of open and biological systems.

Move across the field to turn the torus.

Strange attractor

Unpredictable in detail, ordered in the whole

Self-organising and purposeful, like sociocultural systems that choose their own ends and means. Two runs that start a millionth apart end up worlds apart.

Separation between the two runs 0.000001

Click the field to restart both runs.

3.7Business architecture toolset from the book

BAT®: one golden thread from thinking to action

BAT models any organisation as three nested architectures. Its algorithms trace how the quality of thinking flows into enablers, and how enablers flow into daily action.

  • Strategic architecture

    The type and quality of thinking and planning that precedes any action, whether linear, strategic or futures thinking.

  • Tactical architecture

    The enablers relied on to execute a plan: process, people and technology.

  • Operational architecture

    The daily, authentic actions people take in pursuit of the plan.

Shown at level of resolution 1 of 7. No two organisations are configured alike, so every deployment breaks with silver-bullet thinking.
